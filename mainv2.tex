\begin{abstract}
Ziemiański's cube chain category $\Ch(K)$ is a combinatorial model for the space of directed paths
on a precubical set $K$. Paliga and Ziemiański built a braid invariant for precubical sets
by observing a relation between $\pi_1|\Ch(Z)|$ and braids, where $Z$ is the terminal precubical set.
However, that invariant trivializes for Euclidean $K$ because $|\Ch(\cube n)|$ is
contractible \cite[Prop.~6.1]{PZ}.
This paper constructs a localization of $\Ch(K)$ at morphisms that preserve the order
of coordinates, called $\Br(K)$. And we will show that this structure is the source of the braid
structure of $\pi_1|\Ch(Z)|$. But, there is no nerve or geometric realization in this technique.
Instead we build a 2-polygraph giving an artin braid-like presentation of
$\Br(K)$. The $\{0,1,2\}$-cells of the polygraph are the dimension $\{0,1,2\}$ chains of $K$.
Intuitively, $3$-cells of K contribute braid relations, and pairs of non-intersecting $2$-cells of
$K$ contribute commuting relations. This is not just a metaphor: at $K = Z$,
we get the artin relations and generators exactly. So $\Br(Z)$ is equivalent to the
positive braid category. The presentation is natural in $K$, providing a generalization of the
existing work. As an application, we establish a link to the hyperplane arrangement literature.
The way braids are constructed via salvetti's theorem for the braid arrangement exactly matches
the polygraph we build here for the cube.
All this work is formalized in Lean \cite{Cubical}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Ziemiański's cube chain category $\Ch(K)$ is a combinatorial model for the executions of a
bipointed precubical set $K$ \cite[Thm.~7.6]{Z2}. Paliga and Ziemiański established a homotopy 
equivalence between $|\Ch(Z)|$ and configuration spaces of points in the plane, 
where $Z$ is the terminal precubical set.
It extends, by naturality, to an invariant of any precubical set $K$.
However, it trivializes on any Euclidean complex, see {\cite[Prop.~6.1]{PZ}}. 
The proof is one line: the invariant factors through $|\Ch(\cube n)|$, which is contractible.

This paper explains that the braid structure is present in $\Ch(K)$ without passing through 
any homotopy equivalences. We will construct a set of morphisms, $W$, in $\Ch(K)$. 
And define the localization $\Br(K) \;:=\; \Ch(K)\op\bigl[(W\op)\inv\bigr]$. Then we will build a 
2-polygraph giving an artin-like presentation for $\Br(K)$ in terms of chains of $K$. 
We do not pass via a homotopy equivalence to the path space, as it gives us several advantages. 
Firstly, we preserve a lot of the directed structure of in $K$, so $\Br(\cube n)$ it does not collapse.
Secondly, it permits us to stay with positive braids which yields a simpler theory.

The the cells of our polygraph are straightforward. A chain of $K$ has a list of positive integers
for the dimension of the cubes. Let the degree of a chain be the sum of the list minus the length.
Then the polygraph's $n$-cells are the degree $n$ chains, for $n \in \{0,1,2\}$. The attachements
of the 2 cells to the 1 cells will take some work to formalize, but the following pictures gives 
a clear intuition about the attachements. The key point is that every degree 2 chain has one 
of these forms.

\paragraph{Cube chains \cite{PZ,Z2}.}
The \textbf{wedge} $P \vee Q$ of $P,Q \in \BPSet$ is the pushout in $\pSet$ gluing $\fin_P$ to
$\init_Q$, bipointed by $\init_P$ and $\fin_Q$; $(\BPSet, \vee, \cube 0)$ is monoidal. A
\textbf{shape} is a list $a = [a_1,\dots,a_r]$ of positive integers, with \textbf{serial wedge}
$\bigvee a := \cube{a_1} \vee \cdots \vee \cube{a_r}$ and \textbf{blocks} the $\cube{a_i}$; put
$|a| := \sum_i a_i$ and $\deg a := \sum_i (a_i - 1) = |a| - r$. Then $\Ch(K)$ is the full
subcategory of $\BPSet/K$ spanned by the serial wedges: an object is a bipointed map
$\bigvee a \to K$ and a morphism, a \textbf{refinement}, is a map of wedges commuting over $K$. We
write $\deg d$ and $|d|$ for the degree and event count of a chain's shape and
$\Ch(u) : \Ch(K) \to \Ch(L)$ for the pushforward along $u : K \to L$, which postcomposes the
classifying map and leaves the shape alone. A chain of degree $0$ is a \textbf{run}; the full
subcategory $\Run(K)$ of runs is discrete, a refinement losing no block being an identity.

We use three facts from \cite[\S2]{PZ}: serial wedges are non-self-linked and admit an altitude
function $\alt : K_0 \to \Z$ with $\alt(\fin e) = \alt(\init e) + 1$ on edges, the final vertex of
$\bigvee a$ having altitude $|a|$; a bipointed map between connected objects admitting altitude
preserves it \cite[Prop.~2.7]{PZ}; and $\Ch(P) \times \Ch(Q) \to \Ch(P \vee Q)$, concatenation, is
an isomorphism when $P \vee Q$ is non-self-linked and admits altitude \cite[Lem.~2.11]{PZ}.

\paragraph{The events of a shape.}
Let $\Coord : \Cube \to \Set$ send $\cube n$ to $[n]$ and a face embedding $g$ to the monotone
injection $g_*$ naming the directions $g$ leaves free, and let $E(\Coord)$ be its left Kan
extension along Yoneda. That functor is cocontinuous and $\Coord(\cube 0) = \emptyset$, so the
pushout defining a wedge becomes a coproduct:
\[
  \Ev(a) \;:=\; E(\Coord)\Bigl(\bigvee a\Bigr) \;\cong\; \coprod_i [a_i] .
\]
Write $f_* := E(\Coord)(f)$.

\begin{convention}\label{conv:events}
  We identify $\Ev(a)$ with $[N]$, $N = |a|$, by listing $\coprod_i [a_i]$ block by block, and
  write $\blk_a : [N] \to [r]$ for the resulting block map. A shape on $N$ events is thus a
  partition of $[N]$ into consecutive intervals, and it enters everything below only through
  $\blk_a$. We drop the subscript when the shape is clear.
\end{convention}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:coords}
  Let $f : \bigvee a \to \bigvee b$ in $\BPSet$, with $a$ and $b$ shapes on $N$ and $M$ events.
  Then $N = M$; the map $f_*$ is a permutation of $[N]$, and $f \mapsto f_*$ is injective; and
  there is a monotone $\blk(f)$ with
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:blk-coord}
    \blk_b \circ f_* \;=\; \blk(f) \circ \blk_a ,
  \end{equation}
  the restriction of $f_*$ to each block of $a$ being increasing.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  By the pushout, $f$ is determined by its restrictions to the blocks of $a$, each of which lands
  in a single block of $b$ because $a_i > 0$; that gives $\blk(f)$, equation \eqref{eq:blk-coord}
  and the last clause, and a face embedding is determined by the directions it leaves free, so
  $f_*$ determines $f$. Injectivity of $f_*$ and monotonicity of $\blk(f)$ follow by induction on
  the length of $b$: by \cite[Lem.~2.11]{PZ} a map $\bigvee a \to \cube m \vee \bigvee b'$ splits as
  $f_1 \vee f_2$ over a splitting of $a$, so $f_*$ is a disjoint union and $\blk(f)$ is
  $\blk(f_1) \sqcup (1 + \blk(f_2))$ with $\blk(f_1)$ constant at $1$; injectivity of the left half
  is a second induction, the two images at each glued vertex being separated by the coordinates
  already equal to $1$ there. Finally $f$ preserves altitude \cite[Prop.~2.7]{PZ}, so $N = M$ and
  the injection $f_*$ is a permutation.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Presented categories.}
A \textbf{2-polygraph} $\Pg$ consists of a set $V$ of $0$-cells, families $\Pg_1(x,y)$ and
$\Pg_2(x,y)$ for $x,y \in V$, and two maps
$\src, \tgt : \Pg_2(x,y) \to \{\text{paths } x \to y\}$ into the free category on the $1$-cells;
$\src(\alpha)$ and $\tgt(\alpha)$ are the \textbf{words} of $\alpha$. A morphism of polygraphs is a
map in each dimension commuting with $\src$ and $\tgt$ on the nose, and $\Polygraph$ is the
resulting category. The category $\pres{\Pg}$ \textbf{presented by} $\Pg$ is the free category on
the $1$-cells modulo the congruence generated by $\src(\alpha) = \tgt(\alpha)$, with quotient
functor $q$.

\begin{proposition}[{\cite[Ch.~2]{Polygraphs}}]\label{prop:present-univ}
  Let $\Pg$ be a $2$-polygraph and $C$ a category.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item A functor $\pres{\Pg} \to C$ is the same thing as a map $V \to \Ob C$ together with an
      arrow of $C$ for each $1$-cell, such that the two words of every $2$-cell are sent to the
      same arrow.
    \item Two functors out of $\pres{\Pg}$ that agree on $0$-cells and $1$-cells are equal.
    \item A morphism of polygraphs bijective in every dimension is an isomorphism.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[{\cite[Ch.~I]{GZ}}]\label{prop:loc}
  Let $W$ be a class of morphisms of a category $C$. There is a functor $Q : C \to C[W\inv]$
  inverting $W$ through which every functor inverting $W$ factors uniquely, and two functors out of
  $C[W\inv]$ that agree after $Q$ are equal.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Newman's lemma.}
Write $\mathrm{Hasse}(P)$ for the Hasse quiver of a poset $P$. A \textbf{reading} of $P$ in a
category $C$ assigns an object to each element and an arrow to each covering relation; it extends
to paths by composition.

\begin{theorem}[coherent Newman's lemma; \cite{Huet}, {\cite[Ch.~4]{Polygraphs}}]\label{thm:newman}
  Let $P$ be a finite poset and $\varphi$ a reading of $P$ in $C$. Suppose that for every pair of
  distinct covers $x \lessdot y_1$, $x \lessdot y_2$ and every common upper bound $z$ of $y_1$ and
  $y_2$ there are $j \le z$ and paths $p_i$ from $y_i$ to $j$ with
  $\varphi(x \lessdot y_1)\,\varphi(p_1) = \varphi(x \lessdot y_2)\,\varphi(p_2)$. Then $\varphi$
  sends parallel paths to equal arrows, so it extends uniquely to a functor $P \to C$; and a
  functor out of $P$ is determined by its values on covers.
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:polygon}
  Let $P$ be a finite poset, graded by $r$ with $r(y) = r(x) + 1$ on covers, in which every element
  has exactly two Hasse neighbours. Then a directed path is determined by its first edge; and if
  $P$ has a least and a greatest element, it has exactly two maximal chains, of a common length
  $\ell$, and $|P| = 2\ell$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Undirected, the Hasse graph is $2$-regular, hence a disjoint union of cycles, and $r$ increases
  along a directed path, so a path leaves each vertex by the edge it did not arrive on. Both edges
  at a least element point up and both at a greatest point down, so the cycle through the least
  element meets the greatest one and splits into two directed arcs, of equal length by the grading;
  that cycle is all of $P$, since every element lies on a path from the least to the greatest.
\end{proof}

\section{Squares, crossings, and the braiding}\label{sec:squares}

\gap{One paragraph: a square is a pair of events of one block, and the whole construction runs on
the question of which squares a map reverses.}

Throughout, $a$ and $b$ are shapes on $N$ events, read as partitions of $[N]$ into consecutive
intervals (\Cref{conv:events}).

\begin{definition}\label{def:squares}
  A \textbf{square} of $a$ is a pair $s = (p,q)$ of events with $p < q$ and $\blk(p) = \blk(q)$;
  write $\Sq(a)$ for the set of them, so $|\Sq(a)| = \sum_i \binom{a_i}{2}$. A set
  $R \subseteq \Sq(a)$ orients the complete graph on each block: send $(p,q)$ to the edge
  $q \to p$ when $(p,q) \in R$, and to $p \to q$ otherwise. Call $R$ \textbf{biclosed} when no
  block's tournament has a directed $3$-cycle. On a triple $p<q<r$ of one block the two cycles are
  $p \to r \to q \to p$ and $p \to q \to r \to p$, so this says
  \[
    (p,q) \in R \text{ and } (q,r) \in R \implies (p,r) \in R,
    \qquad
    (p,r) \in R \implies (p,q) \in R \text{ or } (q,r) \in R .
  \]
  We write $\Sq(d)$ for the squares of a chain's shape, and $f_*$ also for the induced injection
  $\Sq(a) \to \Sq(b)$, $(p,q) \mapsto (f_*p, f_*q)$, well defined because $f_*$ is increasing on
  blocks (\Cref{prop:coords}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}\label{def:crossings}
  The \textbf{crossings} of a map of serial wedges $f : \bigvee a \to \bigvee b$ are the inversions
  of the relabelling it performs:
  \[
    \Cross(f) \;:=\; \bigl\{\, (p,q) \;:\; p < q \text{ and } f_*\inv(q) < f_*\inv(p) \,\bigr\} .
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sq-range}
  The map $f_*\inv$ preserves the order of any two events lying in distinct blocks of $b$. Hence
  $\Cross(f) \subseteq \Sq(b)$: the two events of a crossing share a block, so no squareness
  condition need be imposed. Moreover a square $(p,q)$ of $b$ lies in the image of $f_*$ if and
  only if $f_*\inv p$ and $f_*\inv q$ lie in one block of $a$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let $u < v$ lie in distinct blocks of $b$, so $\blk_b(u) < \blk_b(v)$, and put $w = f_*\inv u$,
  $w' = f_*\inv v$. Then \eqref{eq:blk-coord} gives $\blk(f)(\blk_a w) < \blk(f)(\blk_a w')$, so
  $\blk_a(w) < \blk_a(w')$ as $\blk(f)$ is monotone, so $w < w'$ as blocks are intervals. An
  inversion of $f_*\inv$ therefore has both events in one block of $b$. For the last claim, a
  square in the image pulls back to a square, whose events share a block; conversely if the two
  preimages share a block then, $f_*$ being increasing there, they form a square mapping to
  $(p,q)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cocycle}
  For $f : \bigvee a \to \bigvee b$ and $g : \bigvee b \to \bigvee c$,
  \[
    \Cross(gf) \;=\; g_*\bigl(\Cross f\bigr) \;\sqcup\; \Cross(g),
  \]
  a disjoint union, and $\Cross(\id) = \emptyset$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let $s$ be a square of $c$. If $s = g_*(t)$ then $s \notin \Cross(g)$, the two events of $t$
  sharing a block (\Cref{lem:sq-range}) --- which also gives disjointness --- and $(gf)_*\inv$
  agrees with $f_*\inv$ on the events of $t$, so $s \in \Cross(gf)$ if and only if
  $t \in \Cross(f)$. If $s$ is not in the image of $g_*$ then it is not in $g_*(\Cross f)$, and its
  two events pull back along $g_*$ into distinct blocks of $b$, so the second part of
  \Cref{lem:sq-range} applied to $f$ gives $s \in \Cross(gf)$ if and only if $s \in \Cross(g)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:W}
  A refinement $f$ is \textbf{order preserving} when $\Cross(f) = \emptyset$. Write $W = W_K$ for
  the class of order-preserving refinements of $\Ch(K)$, and
  \[
    \Br(K) \;:=\; \Ch(K)\op\bigl[(W\op)\inv\bigr], \qquad [c] := Q(c) .
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:W}
  A refinement $f$ is order preserving if and only if $f_* = \id_{[N]}$. Hence $W$ contains the
  identities; $gf \in W$ if and only if $f \in W$ and $g \in W$; any two order-preserving
  refinements with the same source and target are equal; and $\Ch(u)$ carries $W_K$ into $W_L$, so
  it descends to $\Br(u) : \Br(K) \to \Br(L)$, strictly functorially in $u$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  A permutation with no inversions is the identity, and $\Cross(f)$ is the inversion set of
  $f_*\inv$, so $\Cross(f) = \emptyset$ if and only if $f_*\inv = \id$, that is $f_* = \id$.
  The consequences follow: the second from \Cref{lem:cocycle} with $g_*$ injective, the third
  because $f_*$ determines $f$ (\Cref{prop:coords}), and the last because $\Ch(u)$ changes neither
  shape nor wedge map, with the descent given by \Cref{prop:loc}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Runs over a chain}

\begin{definition}\label{def:runover}
  A \textbf{run over $d$} is a refinement $x : X \to d$ whose source $X$ is a run; write
  $\RunOver(d)$ for the set of these. Since $X$ has shape $[1,\dots,1]$, the permutation $x_*$
  enumerates the events of $d$ in the order $x$ fires them; write $p <_x q$ when
  $x_*\inv(p) < x_*\inv(q)$, the \textbf{firing order} of $x$.
\end{definition}

A refinement out of a run is determined by its map of wedges, the classifying map of the source
being forced; so $\RunOver(d)$ is the set of runs of the wedge $\bigvee a$, and $\Cross(x)$ is the
inversion set of the firing order.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:firing-blocks}
  A run over $d$ fires the blocks of $d$ in order: $\blk(p) < \blk(q)$ implies $p <_x q$. So $x$ is
  a choice of linear order on each block of $d$, and $\blk$ is a monotone surjection from the
  firing order onto the blocks, with interval fibres.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  The blocks of the source are singletons, so $\blk \circ x_* = \blk(x)$, monotone by
  \Cref{prop:coords}. A monotone surjection out of a chain has interval fibres, here the blocks of
  $d$; the only freedom left is the order inside each.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:structure}
  $x \mapsto \Cross(x)$ is a bijection from $\RunOver(d)$ onto the biclosed subsets of $\Sq(d)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Both sides are local to a block (\Cref{lem:firing-blocks}), so fix one. A run restricts there to
  a linear order; its tournament is that order, which has no cycle at all, and $\Cross$ restricts
  to its inversion set. Conversely a biclosed $R$ gives a tournament with no $3$-cycle, hence a
  transitive one --- if $p \to q$ and $q \to r$ then $r \to p$ would close a $3$-cycle --- and a
  transitive tournament is a linear order, whose inversion set is $R$.
\end{proof}

\gap{Note that for a single block this is the classical description of inversion sets, and that
inclusion of them is the right weak order \cite[\S3.1]{BB}; the order theory below is that
statement, block by block.}

We order $\RunOver(d)$ by $x \le y :\!\iff \Cross(x) \subseteq \Cross(y)$, and write $\bot_d$,
$\top_d$ for its least and greatest elements, which exist because $\emptyset$ and $\Sq(d)$ are
biclosed.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:push}
  Let $q : o \to d$ be a refinement. Composition with $q$ is an order embedding
  $\RunOver(o) \to \RunOver(d)$, and
  \[
    \Cross(qx) \;=\; q_*\bigl(\Cross x\bigr) \;\sqcup\; \Cross(q) .
  \]
  Moreover $q\bot_o = \bot_d$ if and only if $q \in W$; for $x \in \RunOver(d)$ the conditions
  $x \in W$, $\Cross(x) = \emptyset$ and $x = \bot_d$ agree; $\bot_X = \id_X$ for a run $X$; and
  $\dom\bot_d = \dom\bot_c$ whenever $c \to d$ lies in $W$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  The formula is \Cref{lem:cocycle}. Since $q_*$ is injective with image disjoint from $\Cross(q)$
  (\Cref{lem:sq-range}), $qx \le qy$ if and only if $x \le y$, and $\Cross(q\bot_o) = \Cross(q)$.
  The remaining equivalences are \Cref{thm:structure}, and the last clause is the formula applied
  to $c \to d$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Steps, covers, and cells}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:steps}
  Call a square $s$ a \textbf{step} of $x \in \RunOver(d)$ when $\Cross(x) \symdiff \{s\}$ is
  biclosed, write $\Steps(x)$ for the set of steps and $x^s$ for the run with
  $\Cross(x^s) = \Cross(x) \symdiff \{s\}$. Then $s$ is a step of $x$ if and only if its two events
  are consecutive in the firing order of $x$, and $|\Steps(x)| = \deg d$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  A block's events are consecutive in the firing order (\Cref{lem:firing-blocks}), so ``consecutive
  in the block'' and ``consecutive overall'' agree. If the two events of $s$ are consecutive,
  toggling $s$ swaps an adjacent pair of a linear order, so the result is again a linear order and
  is biclosed by \Cref{thm:structure}. If a third event of the block fires strictly between them,
  toggling $s$ swaps the pair but not the order of either event against that third one, which would
  then have to lie both before and after the pair.

  So the steps are the covers of the firing order lying inside a block. The firing order is a chain
  on $N$ elements and $\blk$ is a monotone surjection from it onto the $r$ blocks
  (\Cref{lem:firing-blocks}); such a map collapses exactly $N - r = \deg d$ covers.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cover}
  If $x < z$ in $\RunOver(d)$ then some $s \in \Steps(x) \setminus \Cross(x)$ lies in $\Cross(z)$.
  Hence $x \lessdot y$ if and only if $\Cross(y) = \Cross(x) \cup \{s\}$ for such an $s$, in which
  case $y = x^s$; and $\RunOver(d)$ is graded by $|\Cross|$, from $0$ at $\bot_d$ to $|\Sq(d)|$ at
  $\top_d$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  If $z$ agreed with $x$ on every pair covering for $<_x$, then $<_x$ would be contained in $<_z$
  by transitivity, and two linear orders so related are equal, so $z = x$. So some covering pair
  $p \lessdot_x q$ has $q <_z p$. Its two events share a block, since otherwise
  \Cref{lem:firing-blocks} would make every run fire $p$ first, so the pair is a step $s$ of $x$
  (\Cref{lem:steps}). If $s = (p,q)$ then $s \in \Cross(z) \setminus \Cross(x)$, as wanted; if
  $s = (q,p)$ then $s \in \Cross(x) \subseteq \Cross(z)$, which says $p <_z q$, a contradiction.

  Given such an $s$ we have $x \le x^s \le y$, forcing $y = x^s$; conversely two biclosed sets
  differing by one element are a covering pair, by \Cref{thm:structure}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:factorisations}
  Let $x \in \RunOver(d)$. Sending a factorisation $X \to m \to d$ of $x$ to the set of steps of
  $x$ whose two events share a block of $m$ is a bijection onto the subsets of $\Steps(x)$; write
  $m_S$ for the middle at $S$ and $\iota_S : X \to m_S$, $\pi_S : m_S \to d$ for its legs. Then
  $\deg m_S = |S|$, and if $S \cap \Cross(x) = \emptyset$ --- we then say $S$ \textbf{rises} from
  $x$ --- then $\iota_S$ is order preserving.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  A middle $m$ makes the $N$ events into its blocks, which by \Cref{lem:firing-blocks} applied to
  $\iota$ are intervals of the firing order of $x$, and $\pi$ requires each such interval to lie
  inside one block of $d$; conversely such an interval partition arises from the chain whose blocks
  are its parts, in order. The factorisation is determined by that shape: a map of wedges is
  determined by its action on events and injective there (\Cref{prop:coords}), hence a
  monomorphism, while the first leg is forced by which events fall in which part. An interval
  partition of a chain is the same thing as the set of covers it does not cut; those covers lie
  inside a block, so they are steps (\Cref{lem:steps}); and $\deg m$ is $N$ minus the number of
  parts, which is the number of uncut covers.

  Let $S$ rise from $x$. Since $\iota_S$ is a run over $m_S$, its crossings are the squares of
  $m_S$ that $x$ reverses, that is the pairs inside one part that $x$ reverses. The covers of the
  firing order inside a part all lie in $S$, hence none is reversed, so the firing order agrees
  with the event order on consecutive pairs of the part and hence, by transitivity, on every pair
  of it. So $\Cross(\iota_S) = \emptyset$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:cell}
  For runs $X, Y \in \Run(K)$, an \textbf{$n$-cell} from $X$ to $Y$ is a chain $e$ with
  $\deg e = n$, $\dom\bot_e = X$ and $\dom\top_e = Y$; write $\Cell_n(X,Y)$ for the set of them.
\end{definition}

spans, and a $0$-cell is a run.}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:closure}
  Let $S$ rise from $x \in \RunOver(d)$. Then
  \[
    \Cross\bigl(\pi_S \top_{m_S}\bigr) \;=\; \Cross(x) \cup \pi_{S*}\bigl(\Sq(m_S)\bigr),
  \]
  and every $z \ge x$ with $S \subseteq \Cross(z)$ satisfies
  $\pi_{S*}(\Sq(m_S)) \subseteq \Cross(z)$. So $\pi_S\top_{m_S}$ is the least element of
  $\RunOver(d)$ above $x$ reversing all of $S$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \Cref{prop:push} gives $\Cross(\pi_S\top_{m_S}) = \pi_{S*}\Sq(m_S) \cup \Cross(\pi_S)$, and
  \Cref{lem:cocycle} applied to $\pi_S\iota_S = x$ with $\Cross(\iota_S) = \emptyset$ gives
  $\Cross(\pi_S) = \Cross(x)$.

  For the second claim list a part as $p_1 < \cdots < p_k$; as in the previous proof this is also
  its firing order, so each $(p_i,p_{i+1})$ lies in $S$ and is reversed by $z$. Since $\Cross(z)$
  is biclosed, the first clause applied to the triples $(p_i, p_{i+1}, p_j)$ gives, by induction on
  $j-i$, that $z$ reverses every pair of the part.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:covercell}
  Let $x \lessdot y$ in $\RunOver(d)$, with step $s$. Then $\gamma(x \lessdot y) := m_{\{s\}}$ is a
  $1$-cell from $\dom x$ to $\dom y$, with $\pi_{\{s\}}\bot_{m_{\{s\}}} = x$ and
  $\pi_{\{s\}}\top_{m_{\{s\}}} = y$; and every $1$-cell $\alpha$ is $\gamma$ of the unique cover
  $\bot_\alpha \lessdot \top_\alpha$ of $\RunOver(\alpha)$. So $\gamma$, together with
  $x \mapsto \dom x$ on vertices, is a map of quivers from $\mathrm{Hasse}(\RunOver(d))$ to the
  quiver of $1$-cells, and $\gamma(qe) = \gamma(e)$ for every refinement $q : o \to d$ and every
  cover $e$ of $\RunOver(o)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  $m_{\{s\}}$ has degree $1$ and $\dom\bot_{m_{\{s\}}} = \dom x$ by \Cref{prop:factorisations}; by
  \Cref{lem:closure} its top composes forward to the least element above $x$ reversing $s$, namely
  $x^s = y$. Conversely a chain $\alpha$ of degree $1$ has one square, so $\RunOver(\alpha)$ is the
  two-element poset $\bot_\alpha \lessdot \top_\alpha$, and the factorisation through its one step
  is $\alpha$. For the last claim the step of $qe$ is $q_*$ of the step of $e$ (\Cref{prop:push}),
  and $q_*$ carries the firing order of $x$ to that of $qx$, so the two factorisations cut the same
  partition and have the same middle.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The attachment of a 2-cell}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:orderpath}
  Order $\Sq(d)$ lexicographically, by the smaller event and then the larger. Every lower set of
  that order is biclosed, and so is every upper set. Consequently, for $\prec$ either that order or
  its reverse, the runs $x_t$ with $\Cross(x_t)$ the first $t$ squares form a maximal chain
  $\bot_d = x_0 \lessdot \cdots \lessdot x_{|\Sq(d)|} = \top_d$, in which the $(t+1)$-st square is
  a step of $x_t$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  On a triple $p<q<r$ of one block the three squares come in the order
  $(p,q) \prec (p,r) \prec (q,r)$. For a lower set $R$, membership of $(q,r)$ forces that of
  $(p,r)$, and membership of $(p,r)$ forces that of $(p,q)$, which are the two clauses; for an
  upper set, $(p,q) \in R$ forces $(p,r) \in R$ and $(p,r) \in R$ forces $(q,r) \in R$. The chain
  then exists by \Cref{thm:structure}, consecutive terms differ by one element, and
  \Cref{lem:cover} applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:words}
  For a chain $e$ with $\deg e = 2$, let $\src(e)$ and $\tgt(e)$ be the words of $1$-cells read off
  under $\gamma$ from the two chains of \Cref{lem:orderpath}, for the lexicographic order and for
  its reverse.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:polygon}
  Let $\deg e = 2$. Then $\RunOver(e)$ is a polygon on $2|\Sq(e)|$ elements whose only two maximal
  chains from $\bot_e$ to $\top_e$ are those of \Cref{lem:orderpath}; so $\src(e)$ and $\tgt(e)$
  have length $|\Sq(e)|$ and differ in their first letter. Moreover either $e$ has one block of
  dimension $3$, $|\Sq(e)| = 3$ and $\RunOver(e)$ is a hexagon, or $e$ has two blocks of dimension
  $2$, $|\Sq(e)| = 2$ and $\RunOver(e)$ is a square.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Every $x \in \RunOver(e)$ has exactly two steps (\Cref{lem:steps}), and the two flips are its two
  Hasse neighbours, upward or downward according to whether the step is already reversed
  (\Cref{lem:cover}). The two chains of \Cref{lem:orderpath} leave $\bot_e$ by different covers,
  the least and greatest squares being distinct, so \Cref{lem:polygon} applies with the grading
  $|\Cross|$. Finally $\deg e = 2$ forces the block dimensions above $1$ to be $\{3\}$ or
  $\{2,2\}$, and \Cref{def:squares} counts $\binom32 = 3$ and $2\binom22 = 2$.
\end{proof}

The two species are drawn in \Cref{fig:species-sq} and \Cref{fig:species-cube}.
\begin{definition}\label{def:poly}
  The \textbf{Artin polygraph} $\Art(K)$ has $0$-cells the runs of $K$, $1$-cells
  $\Art(K)_1(X,Y) := \Cell_1(X,Y)$, $2$-cells $\Art(K)_2(X,Y) := \Cell_2(X,Y)$, and the words
  $\src$, $\tgt$ of \Cref{def:words}.
\end{definition}

\gap{One paragraph on the name: $\Art(K)$ is built from $K$ alone, with no braid theory in it, and
\Cref{prop:polygon} already exhibits its $2$-cells as braid and commutation relations;
\Cref{thm:C} is what makes the name literal.}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:closes}
  Read in $\pres{\Art(K)}$, the quiver map $\gamma$ satisfies the hypothesis of \Cref{thm:newman}
  on $\RunOver(d)$. So it extends uniquely to a functor
  $\Gamma_d : \RunOver(d) \to \pres{\Art(K)}$, and $\Gamma_d(qx) = \Gamma_o(x)$ for every
  refinement $q : o \to d$ and every $x \in \RunOver(o)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Let $x \lessdot y_1$, $x \lessdot y_2$ be distinct covers with steps $s_1 \neq s_2$, and $z$ a
  common upper bound. Then $S = \{s_1,s_2\}$ rises from $x$, so $\alpha := m_S$ is a chain of
  degree $2$, that is a $2$-cell (\Cref{prop:factorisations}), and $j := \pi_S\top_\alpha$
  satisfies $j \le z$ (\Cref{lem:closure}). By \Cref{prop:polygon} the two maximal chains of
  $\RunOver(\alpha)$ are $\src(\alpha)$ and $\tgt(\alpha)$, and they leave $\bot_\alpha$ by its two
  covers. Composition with $\pi_S$ carries $\bot_\alpha$ to $x$ and $\top_\alpha$ to $j$, carries
  covers to covers (\Cref{prop:push}, \Cref{lem:cover}) and keeps the $1$-cell each cover reads as
  (\Cref{cor:covercell}). So the two chains become two paths from $x$ to $j$, leaving by $y_1$ and
  by $y_2$ and reading as $\src(\alpha)$ and $\tgt(\alpha)$, which are equal in $\pres{\Art(K)}$.

  For the last claim, composition with $q$ is monotone, so $x \mapsto \Gamma_d(qx)$ and $\Gamma_o$
  are functors out of $\RunOver(o)$; they agree on covers by \Cref{cor:covercell}, hence agree, by
  the last clause of \Cref{thm:newman}.
\end{proof}

\section{The presentation}\label{sec:thmA}

\gap{Frame the proof as a comparison of two universal properties: a functor out of
$\pres{\Art(K)}$ is a reading of the degree-one chains identifying the two words of every
degree-two chain (\Cref{prop:present-univ}), and a functor out of $\Br(K)$ is a functor on
$\Ch(K)\op$ inverting $W$ (\Cref{prop:loc}). Each is built from the other.}

A cell $\alpha \in \Cell_n(X,Y)$ comes with the two refinements $\bot_\alpha : X \to \alpha$ and
$\top_\alpha : Y \to \alpha$, of which the first is order preserving; since $Q$ inverts
$\bot_\alpha\op$, we may set
\[
  E_K(X) := [X], \qquad
  E_K(\alpha) \;:=\; Q(\top_\alpha\op) \circ Q(\bot_\alpha\op)\inv \;:\; [X] \to [Y] .
\]

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:telescope}
  Let $d$ be a chain and $z \in \RunOver(d)$. Every path from $\bot_d$ to $z$ in
  $\mathrm{Hasse}(\RunOver(d))$ reads, under $E_K \circ \gamma$, as
  $Q(z\op) \circ Q(\bot_d\op)\inv$. In particular the two words of a $2$-cell $e$ are paths from
  $\bot_e$ to $\top_e$ (\Cref{prop:polygon}), so they have the same image and $E_K$ extends to a
  functor $\pres{\Art(K)} \to \Br(K)$ by \Cref{prop:present-univ}(i).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Induct on the length of the path, the empty path reading as the identity. For a cover
  $x \lessdot y$ with step $s$, put $\beta := \gamma(x \lessdot y)$ and $q := \pi_{\{s\}}$, so
  $x = q\bot_\beta$ and $y = q\top_\beta$ (\Cref{cor:covercell}) and $x\op = \bot_\beta\op q\op$.
  The middle two factors of
  \[
    E_K(\beta) \circ Q(x\op) \circ Q(\bot_d\op)\inv
      \;=\; Q(\top_\beta\op) \circ Q(\bot_\beta\op)\inv \circ Q(\bot_\beta\op) \circ Q(q\op)
            \circ Q(\bot_d\op)\inv
  \]
  cancel, leaving $Q(\top_\beta\op)Q(q\op)Q(\bot_d\op)\inv = Q(y\op)Q(\bot_d\op)\inv$.
\end{proof}


\begin{definition}\label{def:theta}
  Let $\Theta_K : \Ch(K)\op \to \pres{\Art(K)}$ be given by $\Theta_K(d) := \dom\bot_d$ and, for a
  refinement $u : c \to d$,
  \[
    \Theta_K(u\op) \;:=\; \Gamma_d\bigl(\bot_d \le u\bot_c\bigr)
      \;:\; \dom\bot_d \to \dom\bot_c ,
  \]
  which typechecks because $u\bot_c \in \RunOver(d)$ has source $\dom\bot_c$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:theta}
  $\Theta_K$ is a functor, and $\Theta_K(u\op)$ is an identity whenever $u$ is order preserving; so
  $\Theta_K$ descends uniquely to $\Phi_K : \Br(K) \to \pres{\Art(K)}$ with $\Phi_K Q = \Theta_K$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let $v : b \to c$ and $u : c \to d$. Composition with $u$ is monotone (\Cref{prop:push}), so
  $\bot_d \le u\bot_c \le uv\bot_b$, and $\Gamma_d$ being a functor,
  $\Theta_K((uv)\op) = \Gamma_d(u\bot_c \le uv\bot_b) \circ \Theta_K(u\op)$; the left factor is
  $\Gamma_c(\bot_c \le v\bot_b) = \Theta_K(v\op)$ by the last clause of \Cref{prop:closes}. If $u$
  is order preserving then $u\bot_c = \bot_d$ (\Cref{prop:push}), so the comparison is an identity.
  The descent is \Cref{prop:loc}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:A}
  For every bipointed precubical set $K$, the functor $E_K$ is an equivalence
  $\pres{\Art(K)} \to \Br(K)$, with $\Phi_K$ inverse to it.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  On a $0$-cell, $\Phi_K(E_K(X)) = \Theta_K(X) = \dom\bot_X = X$ (\Cref{prop:push}). On a $1$-cell
  $\alpha$, applying $\Phi_K$ to the defining composite gives
  $\Phi_K(E_K(\alpha)) = \Theta_K(\top_\alpha\op) \circ \Theta_K(\bot_\alpha\op)\inv$, whose second
  factor is an identity because $\bot_\alpha$ is order preserving (\Cref{lem:theta}). Since
  $\top_\alpha\bot_Y = \top_\alpha$ and $\RunOver(\alpha)$ is the two-element poset
  $\bot_\alpha \lessdot \top_\alpha$,
  \[
    \Theta_K(\top_\alpha\op) \;=\; \Gamma_\alpha(\bot_\alpha \le \top_\alpha)
      \;=\; q\bigl(\gamma(\bot_\alpha \lessdot \top_\alpha)\bigr) \;=\; q(\alpha)
  \]
  by \Cref{cor:covercell}. So $\Phi_K E_K$ agrees with the identity on $0$- and $1$-cells, hence is
  the identity (\Cref{prop:present-univ}(ii)).

  For the other composite, \Cref{lem:telescope} gives
  $E_K(\Theta_K(u\op)) = Q((u\bot_c)\op)Q(\bot_d\op)\inv
   = Q(\bot_c\op) \circ Q(u\op) \circ Q(\bot_d\op)\inv$, which is $Q(u\op)$ conjugated by the
  isomorphisms $[c] \cong [\dom\bot_c]$ that $Q$ makes of the order-preserving refinements
  $\bot_c$. So $E_K\Theta_K \cong Q$, and $E_K\Phi_K \cong \id$ by uniqueness of descent
  (\Cref{prop:loc}).
\end{proof}

\section{Naturality}\label{sec:thmB}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:B}
  Sending $K$ to $\Art(K)$, and $f : K \to L$ to $\Ch(f)$ on cells in every dimension, is a functor
  $\BPSet \to \Polygraph$; and for every $f : K \to L$,
  \[
    E_L \circ \pres{\Art(f)} \;=\; \Br(f) \circ E_K
    \;:\; \pres{\Art(K)} \longrightarrow \Br(L) .
  \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Write $f_\bullet := \Ch(f)$. A run over a chain $e$ is determined by its map of wedges
  (\Cref{def:runover}), and $f_\bullet e$ has the same shape and the same maps of wedges into it,
  so $\RunOver(e) = \RunOver(f_\bullet e)$ as ordered sets. Everything in \Cref{def:crossings},
  \Cref{lem:steps} and \Cref{cor:covercell} is defined from the map of wedges alone, so $f_\bullet$
  preserves $\bot$, $\top$, cells, steps, covers and $\gamma$; and it carries the lexicographic
  order on $\Sq(e)$ to the lexicographic order on $\Sq(f_\bullet e) = \Sq(e)$, so it preserves
  $\src$ and $\tgt$ letter by letter. Hence $\Art(f)$ is a morphism of polygraphs, functorially in
  $f$ because $\Ch$ is a functor.

  Both composites send a run $X$ to $[f_\bullet X]$. On a $1$-cell $\alpha$, $\Br(f)$ is the
  descent of $Q_L \circ f_\bullet\op$, so it sends $E_K(\alpha)$ to
  $Q((f_\bullet\top_\alpha)\op) \circ Q((f_\bullet\bot_\alpha)\op)\inv$. Here $f_\bullet\bot_\alpha$
  is an order-preserving refinement into $f_\bullet\alpha$ out of a run, hence is
  $\bot_{f_\bullet\alpha}$ (\Cref{prop:push}), and likewise
  $f_\bullet\top_\alpha = \top_{f_\bullet\alpha}$. So both composites send $\alpha$ to
  $E_L(f_\bullet\alpha)$, and \Cref{prop:present-univ}(ii) applies.
\end{proof}

\section{The point}\label{sec:thmC}

Since $Z$ is terminal, every serial wedge has exactly one bipointed map to it and every map of
wedges commutes over it, so $\Ch(Z)$ is the category of shapes and maps of wedges. Write $Z_N$ for
the run of $Z$ on $N$ events. Both $\bot_e$ and $\top_e$ have source a run on $|e|$ events, so
every cell of $Z$ is a loop at $Z_{|e|}$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:cuts}
  For a shape $a$ on $N$ events put $\cut(a) := \{\, k \in [N-1] : \blk_a(k) = \blk_a(k+1) \,\}$,
  and write $\cut(d)$ for the cuts of a chain's shape. For $i \neq k$ in $[N-1]$ let
  $\cox(i,k) := 3$ if $|i-k| = 1$ and $\cox(i,k) := 2$ otherwise.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:cuts}
  $a \mapsto \cut(a)$ is a bijection from the shapes on $N$ events onto the subsets of $[N-1]$,
  with $|\cut(a)| = \deg a$. Hence $\Cell_n(Z_N,Z_N)$ is the set of $n$-element subsets of
  $[N-1]$; write $\sigma_k$ for the $1$-cell with $\cut = \{k\}$. Moreover a chain $e$ of $Z$ with
  $\deg e = 2$ and $\cut(e) = \{i<k\}$ has $|\Sq(e)| = \cox(i,k)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  A shape on $N$ events is a composition of $N$ and $\cut(a)$ is the complement of its set of block
  boundaries in $[N-1]$, so the first claim is the classical correspondence \cite[\S1.2]{EC1} and
  $|\cut(a)| = N - r = \deg a$. For the last claim, $e$ has a block of dimension $3$ exactly when
  three consecutive events share a block, that is exactly when $k = i+1$; then $|\Sq(e)| = 3$, and
  otherwise $|\Sq(e)| = 2$, by \Cref{prop:polygon}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:pos}
  Let $d$ be a chain on $N$ events, $x \in \RunOver(d)$, and $s$ a step of $x$. Let $\pos_x(s)$ be
  the earlier of the two times at which $x$ fires the events of $s$, so $x$ passes $s$ between
  times $\pos_x(s)$ and $\pos_x(s)+1$. Then:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $\pos_x$ is a bijection $\Steps(x) \to \cut(d)$;
    \item $\pos_{x^s}(s) = \pos_x(s)$;
    \item $\pos_{\bot_d}(s) = p$, where $s = (p,q)$.
  \end{enumerate}
  If $d$ is a chain of $Z$, then a cover $x \lessdot y$ with step $s$ has
  $\gamma(x \lessdot y) = \sigma_{\pos_x(s)}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  (i) The fibres of $\blk$ on the firing order are consecutive intervals of lengths
  $a_1,\dots,a_r$ (\Cref{lem:firing-blocks}), independent of $x$, so the set of times $t$ with $t$
  and $t+1$ in one block is $\cut(d)$ for every $x$. A step is a cover of the firing order inside a
  block (\Cref{lem:steps}), so $\pos_x$ lands in $\cut(d)$, and it is injective because distinct
  covers of a chain have distinct lower ends; both sides have $\deg d$ elements (\Cref{lem:steps},
  \Cref{lem:cuts}). (ii) Flipping $s$ exchanges its two times and changes no other. (iii) $\bot_d$
  fires the events in their own order.

  For the last claim, $\gamma(x \lessdot y) = m_{\{s\}}$, whose blocks are the parts of the firing
  order cut at every cover but $s$ (\Cref{prop:factorisations}); so $m_{\{s\}}$ has one block of
  dimension $2$, at times $\pos_x(s)$ and $\pos_x(s)+1$, and edges elsewhere, so its cut set is
  $\{\pos_x(s)\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:alternate}
  Let $e$ be a chain of $Z$ with $\deg e = 2$ and $\cut(e) = \{i<k\}$, and let
  $\bot_e = x_0 \lessdot \cdots \lessdot x_{|\Sq(e)|} = \top_e$ be the chain of
  \Cref{lem:orderpath} for the lexicographic order, with $s_t$ the $(t+1)$-st square. Then
  $\pos_{x_t}(s_t) = i$ for $t$ even and $k$ for $t$ odd, so
  \[
    \src(e) = \sigma_i\sigma_k\sigma_i\cdots, \qquad \tgt(e) = \sigma_k\sigma_i\sigma_k\cdots,
  \]
  both of length $\cox(i,k)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Every $x_t$ has exactly two steps, passed at $i$ and at $k$ (\Cref{lem:pos}(i)).

  For $t = 0$: $\bot_e$ passes $(p,q)$ at $p$ (\Cref{lem:pos}(iii)), and the lexicographic order
  sorts $\Sq(e)$ by the smaller event first. If $e$ is one block of dimension $3$ on events
  $i, i+1, i+2$, its squares in order are $(i,i{+}1)$, $(i,i{+}2)$, $(i{+}1,i{+}2)$, with smaller
  events $i$, $i$, $i+1 = k$; if $e$ is two blocks of dimension $2$ on $\{i,i{+}1\}$ and
  $\{k,k{+}1\}$, they are $(i,i{+}1)$ and $(k,k{+}1)$, with smaller events $i$ and $k$. Either way
  the least square is passed at $i$ and the greatest at $k$.

  For the step, $s_t$ and $s_{t+1}$ are both steps of $x_{t+1}$: the first because a flip of a step
  is a step, the second by \Cref{lem:orderpath}. Now $\pos_{x_{t+1}}(s_t) = \pos_{x_t}(s_t)$
  (\Cref{lem:pos}(ii)), so $\pos_{x_{t+1}}(s_{t+1})$ is whichever of $i$ and $k$ that is not. The
  words follow by the last clause of \Cref{lem:pos}, and their length is $|\Sq(e)| = \cox(i,k)$
  (\Cref{lem:cuts}). The reverse order has the lexicographic order's greatest square first, passed
  at $k$, and the same induction applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:artin-poly}
  \textbf{Artin's braid polygraph} $\ArtBraid$ has one $0$-cell for each $N \in \N$; at $N$, the
  loops $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{N-1}$; and, for each pair $i<k$ in $[N-1]$, one $2$-cell with words
  $\sigma_i\sigma_k\sigma_i\cdots$ and $\sigma_k\sigma_i\sigma_k\cdots$, both of length
  $\cox(i,k)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:C}
  $\Art(Z) \cong \ArtBraid$ in $\Polygraph$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  The runs of $Z$ are the $Z_N$ and every cell of $Z$ is a loop, so both polygraphs are disjoint
  unions over $N$ and it suffices to compare at each $N$. There the $1$-cells are indexed by
  $[N-1]$ and the $2$-cells by the pairs $i<k$ (\Cref{lem:cuts}), and by \Cref{prop:alternate} the
  words of the $2$-cell at $\{i<k\}$ are those of \Cref{def:artin-poly}. The resulting morphism of
  polygraphs is bijective in every dimension, hence an isomorphism
  (\Cref{prop:present-univ}(iii)).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:brZ}
  $\Br(Z) \simeq \BraidPosOp$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  \Cref{thm:A} at $K = Z$ and \Cref{thm:C}.
\end{proof}
